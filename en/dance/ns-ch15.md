# Nāṭyaśāstra, Chapter 15, Verbal representation (vācika) and prosody (chandahśāstra)

Bharata Muni

[Table of Contents](/dance)

← [Chapter 14](/en/dance/ns-ch14) | [Chapter 16](/en/dance/ns-ch16) →

1/ Now I shall proceed to expound the nature of verbal representation (vācika-abhinaya), which was mentioned earlier and which is connected with vowel and consonant sounds.

2/ Words are known as the body of dramatic art. Gestures, costumes, and makeup merely elucidate the meaning of the words.

3/ The śāstras consist of words and rest upon words; therefore there is nothing that lies beyond words. The entire world rests upon words.

4/ Verbal representation covers: nouns, verbs, particles, prepositions, nominal suffixes, compound words (samāsa), euphonic combinations (sandhi), and case endings.

5/ There are two types of recitation: Sanskrit and Prakrit.

6-7/ Sanskrit declamation is characterized by correct pronunciation, clear articulation, and proper observance of grammatical rules. It is defined by various aspects of speech.

Sounds of speech: vowels and consonants

8/ The fourteen sounds -- a, ā, i, ī, u, ū, ṛ, ṝ, ḷ, ḹ, e, ai, o, au -- are called vowels; and the group of sounds beginning with "ka" is called consonants.

9-10/ Consonants are subdivided into voiced and voiceless, velar, labial, dental, cerebral; they are also classified as nasal, sibilant, and palatal.

11/ Of these groups, ga, gha, na, ja, jha, ña, ḍa, ḍha, ṇa, da, dha, na, ba, bha, ma, ya, ra, la and va are voiced, whereas ka, kha, ca, cha, ṭa, ṭha, ta, tha, pa, pha, śa, ṣa, sa and ha -- are voiceless.

12-14/ Velar sounds -- group "ka"; palatal -- group "cha"; cerebral (retroflex) -- group "ṭa"; dental -- group "ta"; labial -- group "pa".

15-16/ By manner of articulation, sounds are classified as occlusives, open, semivowels, and nasals.

17-18/ Sibilants, semivowels, and special sounds such as jihvāmūlīya and visarjānīya form additional categories.

Vowels and their number

19-20/ Vowels form pairs by length, and their quantitative characteristics determine their role in prosody.

Four kinds of words

21-22/ Words are divided into four categories: verbs, nouns, roots, and prepositions. To these are also related particles, suffixes, euphonic combinations, and case endings.

Nouns

23-24/ Nouns have five varieties with case endings, starting with "su". They have a primary meaning and distinctions by gender.

Verbs

25-26/ Verbs are associated with actions in the present and past tenses. They are divided into twenty-five classes with five hundred roots.

Prepositions and particles

27/ Prepositions modify the meanings of verbal roots.

28/ Particles intensify the basic meanings and etymologies of words.

Suffixes and case endings

29-31/ Suffixes develop meanings through elision and combination. Case endings distinguish the meanings of declinable words.

Euphonic combination (sandhi)

32-33/ Sandhi arises where separated vowels or consonants are joined through proximity in words.

Compound words (samāsa)

34/ Samāsa combines several words expressing a single meaning and manifests in six varieties, including tatpuruṣa.

Words in prose and verse

35/ With these rules of grammar, one should compose works by joining words in verse form or freely in prose.

36-37/ Prose words lack systematic combination and ordered syllabic count; they contain only those syllables necessary for expressing meaning.

38/ Poetic words are distinguished by "systematically combined syllables, having feet and caesura," with an ordered quantity.

39-41/ Rhythmic types (chhandas) are syllabic meters with four feet, organized as even (sama), half-even (ardhasama), or uneven (vishama).

42/ There is no word without rhythm and no rhythm without word. Uniting with each other, they illuminate drama.

Triads (gaṇas)

81-84/ Eight triads constitute syllabic meters:

bha -- heavy, light, light;
ma -- heavy, heavy, heavy;
dja -- light, heavy, light;
sa -- light, light, heavy;
ra -- heavy, light, heavy;
ta -- heavy, heavy, light;
ya -- light, heavy, heavy;
na -- light, light, light.

85/ These eight triads, consisting of combinations of heavy and light syllables, form the basis of prosodic analysis.

Heavy syllables and caesura

86-87/ Heavy are syllables ending in long vowels, anusvāra, visarga, or standing before consonant clusters. The caesura (yati) is a pause dividing the foot at a specific place.

Correct couplets and feet

88-89/ Correct couplets (sampat) are defined by an even distribution of syllables. The feet are characterized by a specific alternation of heavy and light syllables.

Three classes of meters

90-102/ Three classes of meters are established:

-- Divine (daiva): from Gāyatrī to Jagatī -- are used in hymns and sacred texts;
-- Human (mānuṣa): from Atijagatī to Atidhṛti -- are used in dramatic compositions;
-- Semi-divine (ardhadaiva): from Kṛti to Utkṛti -- are used on special occasions.

Each class has its own patron deities, colors, and tonalities, corresponding to specific rasas and bhāvas.
