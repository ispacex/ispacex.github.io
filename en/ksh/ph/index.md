## What is this text

**Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam** — "The Heart of Recognition," written by Kṣemarāja (10th–11th centuries),
a disciple of Abhinavagupta. Twenty short sūtras in which the entire Trika is reduced to
the most essential: Consciousness is free and unfolds the universe from itself;
contracting, it becomes a bound being; recognizing itself, it is liberated.
This text is usually recommended as a starting point — it is the most accessible in the entire
tradition.

Twenty sutras without commentary is a table of contents of a teaching, not the teaching itself. All the meaning lies in
the commentary: sutra `Citiḥ svatantrā viśvasiddhihetuḥ` fits into three words, but what
stands behind them, Kṣemarāja explains in a page. He wrote the commentary himself, to
his own sutras.

## Whose translation is this

These pages bring together two works, and they should not be confused.

* **Twenty sūtras** in Russian are available from Gabriel Pradīpaka, on a separate page, and
  are taken from him as is. Translating them anew would mean diverging from him for no
  reason: the same sūtra on the site should not sound in two readings. His footnotes to
  the sūtras have not been carried over here — they are on [his page](https://www.sanskrit-trikashaivism.com/ru/pratyabhijnahrdayam-only-the-20-sutra-s-trika-scriptures-non-dual-shaivism-of-kashmir-ru/541),
  along with his own introduction to the scripture.
* **Kṣemarāja's analysis** is not published in Russian anywhere: at the source it exists
  only in English — 115 thousand characters. It is translated **here**, from that English
  exposition, and is not presented as Gabriel's words.

Sanskrit — devanāgarī and transliteration — has been carried over from the source unchanged.

## How to read these pages

Each part is structured the same way as the source: first all its Sanskrit, then
the same in Russian.

* <span class="pv-sa">Green</span> marks Kṣemarāja’s commentary, <span class="pv-sa pv-src">reddish</span> — the sūtra itself. In the source, the color here
  is as it happens: in the fifth aphorism everything is in red, in the first — almost
  nothing. Exactly the stanza that ends with the aphorism’s number is made red —
  and this is the only edit made to its layout.
* Under each line of Devanagari stands *the same line in IAST transliteration*. In the
  source they run in two blocks — first all the Sanskrit of the section, then all the
  transliteration; here the lines are paired so you don’t have to flip back and forth.
* Next comes the translation. In it, above the Russian word in small type stands
  <ruby>the Sanskrit<rp> (</rp><rt>saṁskṛta</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> — the one
  that in the source stands in parentheses right after. The parentheses haven’t gone anywhere: they are visible when copying and in search.
* A word that has an entry in the **[glossary of terms](/en/ksh/ph/glossary/)** —
  is a link there; from the glossary the links lead back, straight to the paragraphs where the term
  is analyzed most densely.
* Above the wall of Sanskrit — two buttons: take its Devanagari or its
  transliteration in full. The lines alternate, and one script cannot be selected with the mouse.

**Important** (this is the translator's note): everything in the translation that appears *in parentheses in italics* has been added by the translator to complete the phrase. And everything between double dashes, <span class="pv-note">— like this —</span>, is the translator's explanatory clarification, which is not present in the original.

## Translator's introduction

Gabriel Pradīpaka prefaces the analysis with a short introduction. Here it is
translated along with everything else:

> Hello, again Gabriel Pradīpaka. This is simply the commentary that the very author of Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam — namely Kṣemarāja — wrote to this same scripture. Therefore, my introduction to Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam is for the most part also suitable here. This book is very simple in comparison with the main scripture on Pratyabhijñā — the recognition of the Self — that is, with the famous Īśvarapratyabhijñā of Utpaladeva, which requires a powerful mind to unravel its subtleties. But this does not mean that the commentary on Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam will be without its own difficulties. No, at places it is difficult, as is customary for the scriptures of Trika — but in comparison with the aforementioned treatise (and with others like it) it is completely straightforward. It is like a working manual that quickly leads the seeker to Final Liberation. If you want to know your own divinity as easily as possible — this manual is for you. I am not joking. Having read it, you will no longer be able to miss the recognition of the Self, which is also Final Liberation.
>
> And one more thing: I will only translate this scripture, without giving detailed explanations. If you need a thorough explanation, I advise you to buy the book of the venerable Jaideva Singh. That great translator took upon himself the labor of explaining everything in detail.

## Glossary of terms

**[Dictionary](/en/ksh/ph/glossary/)** — 111 entries, gathered from those very
Sanskrit annotations that accompany every Russian word. This book's list is
**its own**, not shared with its neighbors, and this was decided by measurement: out of one hundred twenty entries
in the dictionary of "[Tantrāloka](/en/ksh/ta/glossary/)" **thirty** do not occur here at
all — the entire ritual — while the most frequent word of the "Heart of Recognition," `citi`, is
entirely absent from that dictionary. The "Heart of Recognition" is not a compendium but the shortest
exposition of the whole system, and its words are its own: `saṅkoca` and `vikāsa`,
`pañcakṛtya`, `grāhaka`, `bhūmikā`, `vyāmohitatā`.
