## What is this text

**Tantrasāra** — "The Essence of the Tantras": Abhinavagupta himself (c. 950–1020) condensed his own
"Tantrāloka](/en/ksh/ta/)" into twenty-two chapters, written for the most part in prose.
"Tantrāloka" — thirty-seven chapters in verse and the most extensive work of the tradition; reading it
straight through is nearly impossible, and Abhinavagupta wrote a summary for his own disciples.
He himself says this in the second stanza of the first chapter: "Not all can immerse themselves in the vast Tantrāloka; hear, then, this Tantrasāra, set forth in simple speech."

The same material, but in a manageable form: methods (*upāya*), the paths of time and space,
the thirty-six tattvas, the descent of Power, initiation and ritual. Alongside
the “Paratriśikā-vivaraṇa](/en/ksh/pv/)” — the most difficult text of the tradition — the “Tantrasāra”
reads as something one should have started with.

## Why she lies here

Then, [site search](/search/) searches the pages of this repository, not those of
others. Text left behind a link will not enter the index in any way: a query
for “*abhiṣeka*” or “descent of the Force” simply will not find the “Tantrasāra,” even though it is there.
For it to be found, it must lie here.

We did not make a translation and will not: Gabriel Pradīpaka already has one, and
complete, all twenty-two chapters. Our work was not a translation but a transfer.

## How to read these pages

Each chapter is structured the same way as in the source: first the entire chapter in Sanskrit,
then the same in Russian.

* <span class="pv-sa">Green</span> marks Sanskrit, and beneath each of its lines —
  *the same line in IAST transliteration*. The green here is the same as on
  [/ksh/pv/](/en/ksh/pv/): it marks the words of Abhinavagupta himself, and the “Tantrasāra”
  is his own from the first line to the last. In the source, both walls stand
  apart — first all the Sanskrit of the chapter, then all the transliteration; here
  the lines are paired so you do not have to flip back and forth.
* Next comes the translation. In it, above the Russian word, in small type, stands
  <ruby>the Sanskrit<rp> (</rp><rt>saṁskṛta</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> — the one that in the
  source stands in parentheses right after. The parentheses have not gone anywhere:
  they are visible when copying and in search. A word that has an entry in the **[glossary

  back, straight to the paragraphs where the term is discussed most thoroughly. The

## Glossary of terms

**[Dictionary](/en/ksh/tantrasara/glossary/)** — 144 entries, gathered from those very
Sanskrit annotations that accompany every Russian word. It is shared with the dictionary
of the "[Tantrāloka](/en/ksh/ta/glossary/)": the book is the same, condensed, and there is no need to explain `tattva`
here in one set of words and there in another. Thirty entries are nonetheless its own, and
almost all are ritualistic — for the "Tantrāloka" in Russian there is only the doctrinal
half, while here everything is translated, together with the ritual.

## Translator's introduction

Gabriel Pradīpaka prefaces each chapter with the same introduction. Here it
appears once — twenty-two repetitions of it are needed neither by the reader nor by search:

> This work was written by the great Master Abhinavagupta and represents a
> concise exposition of Tantrāloka. Tantrāloka is the most important and voluminous work
> of the greatest Master of Trika. Abhinavagupta was also the teacher of the eminent Kṣemarāja
> and lived around 975–1025 CE in Kashmir.
>
> This treatise, named Tantrasāra, is a concise encyclopedia of Tantra.
> Since it is a very advanced text in Trika Shaivism, it is not surprising that a neophyte
> will find it very difficult to understand. To begin to comprehend it, the reader's level
> must be that of a true disciple in Trika.
>
> The goal of life is Liberation. True Liberation constitutes the attainment of His
> Svātantrya, or Absolute Freedom. When Svātantrya of the Great Lord is achieved,
> then you see unity in everything, that is, you cease to see duality as
> before.
>
> Keeping the above in mind, now read Tantrasāra and experience the Supreme Delight,
> dear Śiva.

The full introduction — about Abhinavagupta himself and why he wrote this exposition —
remains [at the source](https://www.sanskrit-trikashaivism.com/ru/tantrasara-introduction-trika-scriptures-non-dual-shaivism-of-kashmir-ru/919).

Two corrections were made, and both were in the Sanskrit, not in the translation. In chapter 6, the chapter heading in Devanagari appears twice in the source; in chapter 10, the heading of chapter 9 comes first (*navamamāhnikam* instead of *daśamamāhnikam*); the extra one was removed, otherwise the lines of Sanskrit and transliteration would have diverged by one paragraph and would not have aligned further on. The title of chapter 2 was taken from the source's table of contents (*Anupāyaprakāśanam*), not from its own introduction, where the title of the first chapter is repeated.
